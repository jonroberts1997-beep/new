    The Thriving Garden Checklist

    Not a to-do list — a way of seeing.

    • Walk regularly and look closely. A garden you walk through three times a week reveals problems while they're small and easy to fix. A garden you visit once a week or less surprises you with problems that have already gotten out of hand.
    • Mix annual herbs and flowers into your vegetable plantings. Basil, dill, cilantro, marigolds, nasturtiums — these attract beneficial insects that prey on the pests that eat your crops. A monoculture of vegetables is an invitation for problems; a diverse planting is a working ecosystem.
    • Allow some vegetable crops to flower. When a carrot or a cilantro plant bolts and flowers, it becomes one of the best sources of food for beneficial insects in your whole garden. Not every bolting plant is a failure — some are doing important work.
    • Let perennial herbs flower. Thyme, oregano, sage, and chives in flower are pollinator magnets and pest-predator habitat. Don't deadhead everything.
    • Grow your most important crops in more than one location where possible. If a pest or disease hits one bed, it won't wipe out your entire supply.
    • Allow a patch of nettles or grasses to grow wild somewhere at the edge of your garden if you can. This is habitat for beneficial insects and the ecology that makes a garden self-regulating over time.
    • Succession plant when possible. When one planting finishes, something goes in its place. An empty bed for more than a few weeks is a missed opportunity and a weed incubator.
